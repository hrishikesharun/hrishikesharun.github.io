Final-year biomedical engineer working where healthcare meets machine intelligence.

I came at this from the clinical side, not the software side. The most useful thing I have done was not writing code. It was standing in an ISO cleanroom in Chennai checking diagnostic consumables against heat marks and cap locking, while a commissioning task force fought a moulding line into producing something repeatable. A device is not finished when the design is finished. It is finished when it survives manufacturing, validation and a regulator, and that is the half most engineers never see.

So I went and learned how healthcare actually pays for things, through Penn's Business of Health Care programme: care delivery economics, payment models, value-based care. It reframes the work. The best analysis in the world does not get adopted if nobody can say who benefits and who gets billed, and that constraint decides what is worth building long before any of it becomes a technical question.

What I build sits on top of that. AI systems that never act on their own: every action runs through deterministic code and a human approval gate, because in a clinical setting an autonomous action is a liability rather than a feature. Clinical signal processing. The analytics layer that has to be legible to the person actually making the decision, who is a clinician and not an analyst. My final year is focused on neural signal processing, applying machine learning to Deep Brain Stimulation recordings, and I graduate from Reading in July 2027.
